\chapter{Requirements Analysis and System Design}

\section{Introduction}
This chapter translates the business framing and technical choices established in the previous chapters into a structured design baseline for \projectname{}. It identifies what the platform was required to do, the constraints imposed by the deployment environment, the principal actors and consumers, and the way the implemented multi-layer architecture satisfies those needs. Whereas Chapter III justified the technology choices, this chapter formalizes the requirements and architectural organization of the completed solution. The design follows established data-intensive engineering principles: separation of concerns, explicit data-movement boundaries, and layered coordination between storage, compute, metadata, and access services \cite{kleppmann2017ddia,armbrust2021lakehouse}.

\section{Requirement Elicitation Context}
The requirements of \projectname{} were derived from three complementary sources:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the business objective of creating a distributed customer-data foundation under the broader \initiative{} vision;
    \item the engineering need to ingest, store, process, validate, orchestrate, expose, and monitor heterogeneous customer-related datasets;
    \item the operational reality of a local control plane connected to three resource-constrained Linux virtual machines with integrated security hardening requirements.
\end{itemize}

The resulting requirements are therefore traceable to implemented capabilities, acceptance criteria, and the final distributed deployment model rather than being presented as abstract intentions.

\section{Functional Requirements}
The functional scope of the platform is centered on the end-to-end lifecycle of customer-related data, from ingestion to governed analytical access. The most important functional requirements are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:chapter4-functional-part1} and Table~\ref{tab:chapter4-functional-part2}.

The requirements were organized as a dependency-aware execution contract rather than as an independent list of features. FR-1 to FR-5 define the data foundation: source-aware ingestion, replayable Kafka transport, durable HDFS bronze persistence, distributed Spark loading, and shared metadata governance. FR-6 to FR-10 cover the controlled use and operation of that foundation through analytical querying, layered transformation, explicit quality validation, orchestration, and observability. This grouping reflects the implemented lifecycle because downstream capabilities are meaningful only after the preceding storage, processing, and catalog conditions have been satisfied.

\pagebreak
Each acceptance criterion is also expressed through an observable technical result. The report therefore distinguishes between a service being reachable and a platform capability being completed: successful ingestion must leave routed records and bronze artifacts, raw loading must produce registered Iceberg tables, transformation must materialize declared models, and validation must retain inspectable evidence. This evidence-oriented formulation supports the traceability analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:requirements-traceability} and provides a consistent basis for the validation campaign presented in Chapter~VIII.

\section{Logical Layered Architecture}
At the logical level, \projectname{} is organized as a layered customer-data platform rather than as a monolithic data pipeline. This organization is aligned with lakehouse thinking, where raw persistence, structured table layers, metadata governance, and analytical access are coordinated but not collapsed into one opaque runtime block \cite{armbrust2021lakehouse}. Each layer has a precise responsibility in the end-to-end flow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ingestion layer:} receives and transports the raw input datasets through Kafka.
    \item \textbf{Bronze persistence layer:} stores replayable raw artifacts in HDFS.
    \item \textbf{Raw lakehouse layer:} structures the raw persisted inputs through Spark and Iceberg.
    \item \textbf{Metadata layer:} centralizes table definitions through Hive Metastore.
    \item \textbf{Raw-validation layer:} checks the source-specific raw lakehouse tables through Great Expectations and persists evidence.
    \item \textbf{Transformation layer:} applies modeled transformations and tests through dbt-spark.
    \item \textbf{Analytical access layer:} exposes governed raw and curated tables through Trino.
    \item \textbf{Cross-cutting orchestration and observability layer:} coordinates execution through Airflow and supervises the platform through Prometheus and Grafana.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-to-End Data Flow}
The end-to-end operational flow of the platform can be summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Airflow initiates the five implemented workflows for environment reset, lakehouse setup, service readiness, integrated raw ingestion/loading and validation, and dbt transformation;
    \item source datasets are prepared and published through the configured ingestion path;
    \item Kafka receives the records and distributes topic partitions across the broker cluster;
    \item bronze consumers persist replayable raw artifacts in HDFS through NameNode/DataNode coordination;
    \item Spark reads the bronze zone and loads Iceberg raw tables through distributed execution, with Hive Metastore registering the shared table definitions;
    \item within the same Kafka--HDFS--Spark workflow, Great Expectations assets are bootstrapped and the raw lakehouse tables are validated, producing JSON results and HTML Data Docs;
    \item dbt-spark creates staging, intermediate, and analytics models and applies its declared transformation tests;
    \item Trino exposes distributed SQL access to the governed raw and curated lakehouse tables;
    \item Prometheus and Grafana continuously expose infrastructure and pipeline monitoring signals across the execution lifecycle.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Design Rationale and Trade-offs}
The final design reflects deliberate engineering trade-offs rather than maximum-complexity choices:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.18em,topsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Distributed execution within constrained resources:} real multi-node cooperation is retained within the available VM resource limits.
    \item \textbf{Layered responsibilities:} explicit service boundaries improve maintainability and fault isolation.
    \item \textbf{Integrated hardening:} deployed paths are protected without implying an enterprise-complete security program.
    \item \textbf{Operational transparency:} checks, metrics, logs, and execution artifacts keep platform behavior inspectable and diagnosable.
    \item \textbf{Future-ready scope:} curated outputs support later Customer 360 and AI services without overstating current functionality.
\end{itemize}

These trade-offs align the architecture with the implemented workload, available infrastructure, and operational requirements.
