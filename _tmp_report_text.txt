Abstract
Modern organizations generate large volumes of customer-related data across heterogeneous
andfragmentedoperationalenvironments.Campaignrecords,behavioraltraces,commercial
transactions, and profile attributes are produced in different formats, at different rates, and
under inconsistent semantic assumptions. When these sources remain isolated, customer
information becomes difficult to validate, reconcile, replay, and transform into dependable
analytical assets. This fragmentation slows decision-making, prevents a unified customer
view, and deprives future intelligent services of a sufficiently reliable data foundation.
This final-year project addresses that problem through the design and implementation of
AdOptimizer CDP, a distributed customer-data lakehouse platform developed within the
broader AdOptimizer AI initiative at SMART AUTOMATION TECHNOLOGIES. The
completed platform combines replayable Kafka ingestion, HDFS bronze storage, Spark
distributed processing, Iceberg and Parquet tables governed through Hive Metastore, and
analytical access through Trino. dbt-spark structures the transformation layers, Great
Expectations records validation evidence, Airflow orchestrates the workflows, Prometheus
and Grafana provide observability, and layered security controls protect service identity,
access, communication, and persisted runtime data.
Deployed across a local control plane and three Linux virtual machines, the platform was
validated through end-to-end execution scenarios covering ingestion, distributed persis-
tence, transformation, cross-engine analytical access, data-quality checks, orchestration,
monitoring, and secure service interaction. The resulting architecture strengthens repro-
ducibility, scalability, traceability, operational reliability, and extensibility. It transforms
heterogeneous raw datasets into governed and reusable data products while establishing
the engineering foundation for trusted analytics, future Customer 360 capabilities, and
subsequent machine-learning developments.
Keywords:Big Data, Data Engineering, Distributed Systems, Data Lakehouse, Customer Data Platform, Data
Governance, Data Quality, Observability, Security, Customer 360.

Résumé
Les organisations modernes génèrent d’importants volumes de données clients dans des
environnements opérationnels hétérogènes et fragmentés. Les données de campagne, les
traces comportementales, les transactions commerciales et les attributs de profil sont
produits dans des formats différents, à des cadences variables et selon des hypothèses
sémantiques non homogènes. Lorsque ces sources demeurent isolées, les informations clients
deviennent difficiles à valider, à réconcilier, à rejouer et à transformer en actifs analytiques
fiables. Cette fragmentation ralentit la prise de décision, empêche une vision client unifiée
et prive les futurs services intelligents d’un socle de données suffisamment robuste.
Ce projet de fin d’études répond à cette problématique par la conception et la mise en
œuvre de AdOptimizer CDP, une plateforme lakehouse distribuée consacrée aux données
clients et développée dans le cadre de l’initiative plus large AdOptimizer AI au sein de
SMART AUTOMATION TECHNOLOGIES. La plateforme mise en œuvre combine une
ingestion rejouable avec Kafka, un stockage bronze dans HDFS, des traitements distribués
avec Spark, des tables Iceberg et Parquet gouvernées par Hive Metastore, ainsi qu’un accès
analytique via Trino. dbt-spark structure les couches de transformation, Great Expectations
conserve les preuves de validation, Airflow orchestre les workflows, Prometheus et Grafana
assurent l’observabilité, tandis que des contrôles de sécurité en couches protègent l’identité
des services, les accès, les communications et les données persistées.
Déployée entre un plan de contrôle local et trois machines virtuelles Linux, la plateforme a
èté validée au moyen de scénarios d’exécution de bout en bout couvrant l’ingestion, la persis-
tance distribuée, la transformation, l’accès analytique inter-moteurs, les contrôles de qualité,
l’orchestration, la supervision et les interactions sécurisées entre services. L’architecture
obtenue renforce la reproductibilité, la scalabilité, la traçabilité, la fiabilité opérationnelle
et l’extensibilité. Elle transforme des jeux de données brutes hétérogènes en produits de
données gouvernés et réutilisables, tout en ètablissant le socle d’ingénierie nécessaire à des
analyses fiables, à de futures capacités Customer 360 et à des développements ultérieurs
en apprentissage automatique.
Mots-clés :Big Data, Ingénierie des données, Systèmes distribués, Data Lakehouse, Plateforme de données client,
Gouvernance des données, Qualité des données, Observabilité, Sécurité, Customer 360.

II.2.2 Vertical Scaling vs Horizontal Scaling
One of the first decisions in large-scale systems concerns how growth should be absorbed.
Vertical scaling strengthens a machine by increasing CPU, memory, or storage capacity.
It is often simpler in the short term because the system does not need to be partitioned
immediately. Nevertheless, it has two major limits: physical growth is finite, and dependence
on a small number of powerful machines weakens resilience.
Horizontal scaling increases capacity by adding nodes. It is more complex because it
requires workload distribution, coordination, and failure management. However, it offers
better long-term elasticity and better alignment with modern data engineering workloads.
For a distributed customer-data platform, horizontal scaling is therefore a structural
architectural principle rather than a simple performance technique [22].
A simplified capacity model is:
Ctotal(n)≈n×Cnode×η(n),0<η(n)≤1
where n is the number of participating nodes,Cnode is the average capacity of one node,
and η(n)is an efficiency factor accounting for coordination, serialization, network transfer,
and workload imbalance. Horizontal capacity therefore rarely grows perfectly linearly, but

additional nodes can still increase total capacity when the workload is partitionable.
II.2.3 Reliability, Scalability, and Maintainability
The literature on distributed systems frequently emphasizes three central qualities: reli-
ability, scalability, and maintainability [22]. Reliability refers to the system’s ability to
continue providing correct service despite component failures, abnormal workloads, or
operational disturbances. Scalability refers to the platform’s capacity to grow without dis-
proportionate degradation or redesign. Maintainability refers to the ability to understand,
operate, extend, and debug the system over time.
These qualities are fundamental for a platform such as AdOptimizer CDP. An unreliable
platform cannot support trusted analytical reuse. A non-scalable platform cannot absorb
new datasets or future use cases. A non-maintainable platform becomes fragile and difficult
to operate. These qualities are therefore direct evaluation criteria for the engineering
relevance of the project.
II.2.4 Data-Intensive Systems as a Distinct Engineering Problem
Data-intensive systems differ from ordinary software systems because data movement,
storage, replayability, and consistency become first-class engineering concerns. In such
systems, correctness is not defined only by the logic of business functions. It also depends
on whether data is durably persisted, whether transformations are reproducible, whether
schemas remain interpretable over time, and whether the system can explain the origin
and lifecycle of the information it exposes.
This distinction is important for the positioning of the present work. AdOptimizer CDP is
not simply an application with a database behind it. It is a distributed data engineering
platform whose value lies in organizing the lifecycle of heterogeneous customer-related
data in a reliable, observable, and governable way.
II.3 Core Theoretical Trade-offs in Distributed Data
Engineering
II.3.1 The CAP Theorem and Consistency Trade-offs
The CAP theorem formalizes a specific constraint: when a network partition prevents nodes
from communicating, a distributed system cannot guarantee both linearizable consistency
and availability for every request [18]. Partition tolerance is not a freely selectable option
10

===== PAGE BREAK =====

AdOptimizer CDP Chapter II. State of the Art and Theoretical Background
once communication failures are possible; the practical decision is how each service behaves
during such failures.
Different layers of a data platform may therefore adopt different consistency and availability
policies. Event transport, distributed storage, metadata services, and analytical table layers
are not expected to behave identically. CAP does not prescribe one platform-wide choice;
it explains why guarantees must be evaluated per service and per operation.
II.3.2 ACID vs BASE Models
ACID describes transactional guarantees: atomicity, consistency, isolation, and durability.
These properties are important when a group of operations must be committed or rejected
as one unit and concurrent updates must preserve defined invariants. However, not every
stage of a distributed data platform requires the same transactional scope or isolation
level.
BASE, commonly summarized as Basically Available, Soft state, and Eventual consistency,
is a design philosophy that accepts temporary divergence while replicas converge. ACID and
BASE are not symmetric or mutually exclusive alternatives: one characterizes transaction
guarantees, while the other describes a broader availability-oriented operating model.
A modern data platform may combine eventual convergence in one service with ACID
transactions in another, according to the responsibilities of each layer [22].
II.3.3 Data Partitioning and Replication
Partitioning divides data into smaller units so that storage and computation can be
distributed across multiple nodes. It is one of the foundational mechanisms behind modern
large-scale platforms. Without partitioning, a single node becomes a natural bottleneck
for read throughput, write throughput, fault recovery, and transformation execution.
Replication complements partitioning by improving resilience and availability. When
several copies of data or logs exist, the system can continue operating even if one node is
unavailable. Yet replication also increases coordination and storage cost, which is why it
must be treated as an architectural decision rather than a simple implementation detail.
The storage cost of replication can be summarized as:

II.3.4 Fault Tolerance and Service Resilience
In distributed systems, failure must be considered normal rather than exceptional. Nodes
may disappear, ports may become temporarily unavailable, certificates may expire, services
may start in the wrong order, and storage volumes may create cascading readiness issues.
A resilient platform therefore needs explicit strategies for retries, health checks, replication,
recovery, and replay.
This principle is especially relevant in data engineering because failures affect not only
uptime but also trust in data products. A silent ingestion failure may create missing data,
while a metadata failure may make tables unreadable. Fault tolerance is therefore directly
tied to platform credibility and reproducibility.
II.3.5 Batch Processing vs Stream Processing
Batch processing is based on finite datasets processed periodically or on demand. It is
suitable for historical backfills, curated analytical transformations, and reproducible large-
scale recomputation. Stream processing is based on continuous flows of records or events
that are handled incrementally as they arrive. It is useful when ingestion or propagation
must remain closer to real-time behavior.

before it is stored in an authoritative analytical repository. This approach improves control
and downstream semantic stability, but it may reduce ingestion flexibility. Schema-on-read,
by contrast, stores raw or semi-structured data first and interprets structure later when
the data is processed or queried.

II.4 Big Data Foundations
II.4.1 The Five Vs of Big Data
Big Data was classically characterized through volume, velocity, and variety [24]. Contem-
porary discussions commonly extend this model with veracity and value. Volume refers to
the quantity of data under management; velocity to the rate at which it is generated and
moved; variety to differences in format, structure, and semantics; veracity to reliability
and quality; and value to its usefulness for decisions and downstream services.
These dimensions show that data-engineering complexity does not depend on volume alone.
In AdOptimizer CDP, variety, veracity, replayability, and reuse pressure are as important
as raw scale, while distribution supports role separation, horizontal evolution, resilience,
and explicit multi-node execution.
II.4.2 Heterogeneous Data Sources and Data Variety
Customer understanding rarely comes from a single operational system. It may involve
campaign response datasets, online behavioral signals, e-commerce or retail transactions,
session-level intent indicators, and profile-related attributes. Each source differs in format,
cadence, completeness, and downstream meaning. This heterogeneity makes integration
difficult because technical normalization is not sufficient; semantic reconciliation is also
required.
As a result, variety becomes both a storage problem and a modeling problem. The platform
must ingest different raw forms, persist them durably, and later organize them into
analytical layers without erasing provenance.
II.4.3 Distributed Storage Principles
Distributed storage spreads persisted data across multiple nodes instead of concentrating
it on a single machine. Its goals include capacity growth, resilience, replication, parallel
read/write support, and better alignment with distributed compute. An important conse-
quence of distributed storage is that it separates durable raw persistence from the compute
jobs that later transform the data.

This separation has strong methodological value. When raw data is durably preserved,
pipelines become replayable and easier to audit. Transformations can be rerun without
repeatedly depending on the original upstream source.
II.4.4 Distributed Compute Principles

nodes. Instead of one machine reading and transforming an entire dataset, work is split
according to partitions, files, or execution stages. This can reduce total execution time
and align compute more closely with distributed storage [22].

overhead. Parallel execution is therefore most useful when the work can be partitioned

II.5 Evolution of Analytical Data Architectures
II.5.1 Traditional Data Warehouse Architecture
The traditional data warehouse is organized around structured, curated, and strongly
modeled analytical data. It is highly effective for stable reporting, dimensional modeling,
and SQL-based decision support. Its main strength is semantic control.
Its limitation appears when heterogeneous or semi-structured data must be ingested rapidly
and preserved before full normalization. In such contexts, the warehouse-only model can
become too rigid because it expects analytical structure too early in the lifecycle.
II.5.2 Data Lake Architecture
The data lake emerged as a response to the rigidity of traditional warehouses. It enables
large quantities of raw and semi-structured data to be stored before full modeling. This
increases ingestion flexibility and supports exploratory reuse across different downstream
consumers.
However, data lakes also introduced a risk: if metadata, naming discipline, and validation
remain weak, the platform can degrade into an opaque storage zone with low analytical
trust. The lake model solves flexibility problems but can create governance problems.
II.5.3 Data Lakehouse Architecture
The data lakehouse seeks to combine the flexibility of the lake with the transactional and
governance discipline associated with warehouses. It preserves distributed and replayable
storage logic while adding stronger table semantics, better schema evolution control, and
improved analytical exposure [11].
This model is particularly well adapted to projects such as AdOptimizer CDP. It allows
raw datasets to be preserved in distributed storage while curated datasets are exposed
through governed tables suitable for transformation frameworks and SQL query engines.
II.5.4 Lambda Architecture
Lambda architecture organizes processing through two paths: a batch path for full historical
recomputation and a speed layer for low-latency updates. Its objective is to reconcile
correctness and freshness. The model was influential because it formalized the difficulty of
satisfying both needs through a single pipeline.

Nevertheless, Lambda architecture is often criticized for operational duplication. Two
processing logics must be maintained, monitored, and reconciled. Its academic relevance
remains high because it helps explain why later architectures searched for simpler ways to
preserve freshness without multiplying engineering burden.
II.5.5 Kappa Architecture
Kappa architecture reduces the conceptual duplication of Lambda by emphasizing a stream-
centered approach in which the event log becomes the main source of truth. Reprocessing
is achieved through replay rather than through a separate batch computation layer [23].
This model is attractive when event logs are rich and replay can serve as the main recom-
putation mechanism. However, many analytical platforms still require clearly governed
historical layers and structured table abstractions. Kappa is therefore instructive, but not
automatically sufficient for every customer-data use case.
II.5.6 Comparative Analysis of These Architectures
The historical movement from warehouses to lakes, from lakes to lakehouses, and from
isolated reporting toward event-aware architectures reflects a broader shift in priorities.
Early architectures privileged control and stable relational semantics. Later architectures
privileged ingestion flexibility and scale. Contemporary platforms increasingly seek a
hybrid answer that preserves raw fidelity, enables distributed processing, and still produces
governed analytical products.
For AdOptimizer CDP, the lakehouse model is the most suitable. It supports raw bronze
persistence, distributed storage, structured analytical tables, metadata-driven reuse, and
downstream SQL exploration. The selected architecture is therefore a reasoned synthesis
of the state of the art.
The implemented project does not reproduce either Lambda or Kappa architecture in a
pure textbook form. It does not maintain a separate low-latency speed layer alongside
a batch layer, as a strict Lambda design would require. Nor does it treat a streaming
computation path as the sole mechanism for producing analytical state, as a strict Kappa
design would suggest. Instead, it combines replayable event transport with durable raw
history and batch-oriented refinement into governed analytical tables. The resulting model
is therefore best described as an event-aware, batch-oriented lakehouse rather than as a
complete Lambda or Kappa implementation [25, 23, 11].

II.6 Foundational Concepts for Governance and Trust
in Data Platforms
II.6.1 Metadata, Cataloging, and Data Discovery
Metadata gives meaning to stored data by describing structure, origin, semantics, and
technical location. Without metadata, even accessible data can remain practically unusable.
Cataloging extends this principle by making datasets discoverable and interpretable by
different actors [22].
In a distributed customer-data platform, metadata is especially important because the
architecture spans several services and layers. Governance cannot depend on informal
memory alone. Schema registration, cataloging, and dataset discovery must therefore be
explicit platform capabilities.
II.6.2 Data Lineage and Traceability
Lineage refers to the ability to trace how data moved from source to output, which
transformations were applied, and which downstream assets depend on which upstream
artifacts. Traceability is essential for reproducibility, debugging, and auditability.
17

Lineage distinguishes an integrated data engineering platform from a collection of discon-
nected scripts. When dependencies and transformations can be reconstructed, the platform
becomes more explainable, auditable, and maintainable.
II.6.3 Data Quality Dimensions
Data quality is multidimensional. Common dimensions include completeness, consistency,
validity, accuracy, timeliness, and uniqueness. These dimensions are especially important in
customer-data contexts because downstream segmentation, profiling, or future predictive
analytics become weak when source data is inconsistent or poorly governed.
Modern platforms increasingly formalize quality as an explicit layer of the architecture
rather than as a purely manual cleaning activity. Checks, expectations, thresholds, and
validation artifacts transform quality into a monitored engineering property.
For example, a simple completeness metric can be written as:
Completeness= Observed non-missing required values
Expected required values
This ratio intentionally measures presence rather than correctness: validity and accuracy
require separate rules. Even a basic completeness measure is useful because it converts a
qualitative concern into a control signal that can be monitored over time.
II.6.4 Observability in Distributed Data Systems
Observability is the capacity to infer the internal state of a system through emitted
evidence such as metrics, logs, traces, health checks, and runtime indicators. In distributed
environments, observability is crucial because failures often emerge from the interaction
between components rather than from one isolated service [29].
For data engineering, observability is tightly linked to platform trust. If the system
cannot show whether ingestion succeeded, whether storage is healthy, whether services
are reachable, or whether transformations produced expected results, operators cannot
diagnose failures or establish confidence in the resulting data products.

II.7 Security Foundations for Distributed Data Plat-
forms
II.7.1 Authentication in Distributed Systems
Authentication establishes who a user or service is. In distributed environments, it becomes
critical because communication crosses process, host, and network boundaries. Storage
services, metadata layers, and query endpoints must be able to distinguish legitimate
principals from unauthorized ones [26].
Without strong authentication, a distributed platform depends on implicit trust between
components. Authentication is therefore a structural foundation of secure distributed data
engineering.
II.7.2 Authorization and Access Control
Once identity is established, the system must determine which actions are permitted.
Authorization controls access to services, datasets, administrative operations, and query
interfaces. It operationalizes the principle of least privilege by restricting each actor to the
minimum access necessary for its role.
In data platforms, authorization cannot be limited to end-user interfaces alone. It also
applies to service-to-service calls, operational scripts, metadata access, and internal gate-
ways.
II.7.3 Encryption in Transit and Encryption at Rest
Encryption in transit protects data while it moves between nodes, services, or users. It
reduces exposure to interception and helps preserve confidentiality across the network.
Encryption at rest protects persisted data on storage media and reduces exposure if storage
devices or mounted runtime paths are accessed outside intended controls [13].
These mechanisms address different threat surfaces and are complementary. In a multi-
service lakehouse platform, transport protection reduces network exposure, while at-rest
encryption protects persisted runtime volumes when storage is accessed outside the intended
execution path.

II.7.4 Secure Service Exposure in Multi-Node Environments
Distributed platforms expose numerous service endpoints: orchestration UIs, query inter-
faces, storage consoles, monitoring dashboards, and service APIs. If these are exposed
without proxying, credential checks, and transport protection, the system accumulates a
wide attack surface [27, 29].
Secure exposure therefore requires deliberate control of entry points, certificates, internal-
versus-external interfaces, and service-access boundaries.

AdOptimizer CDP aligns with the theoretical principles developed in this chapter through

III.3.1 Event Ingestion and Distributed Bronze Storage
Kafka was retained as the ingestion backbone because its partitioned-topic model maps
directly to the project’s dataset-specific transport and three-broker topology [7]. RabbitMQ
providesmaturequeueandstreamabstractions,whilePulsarprovidesdistributedmessaging
with multiple subscription models [12, 9]. Both are credible alternatives, but adopting
either would have introduced a different operational model without improving the required
Kafka-to-HDFS replay path already implemented in the platform.
HDFS was selected for the bronze landing zone because its NameNode/DataNode archi-
tecture and configurable block replication make distributed persistence directly observable
across the three VMs [3]. Local or shared folders would not demonstrate distributed storage,
while object storage would provide a different access abstraction and would weaken the
intended Hadoop-centered deployment demonstration. HDFS therefore serves both an
engineering purpose—replayable raw persistence—and an architectural purpose by proving
that bronze data is physically managed by a distributed file system.
III.3.2 Lakehouse Tables, Metadata, and File Format
Apache Iceberg manages the structured raw and curated lakehouse tables, Hive Metastore
centralizes their discoverable metadata, and Parquet provides the underlying columnar file
representation [6, 4, 8]. This separation is important: Parquet defines how analytical data
is stored in files, Iceberg adds table-level metadata and evolution capabilities, and Hive
Metastore exposes shared catalog definitions to compatible engines.
Delta Lake and Apache Hudi were considered as alternative lakehouse table technologies
[16, 5]. Iceberg was retained because the implemented project required a neutral table layer
shared by Spark and Trino, with explicit schema and partition evolution rather than a
platform-specific managed environment. The choice also aligns with the report’s objective
of making the relationship between physical files, table metadata, and the external catalog
academically visible.
III.3.3 Distributed Processing, Querying, and Transformation
Spark is the main distributed compute engine for bronze-to-raw loading and curated
transformation execution. Its standalone master/worker model maps naturally to VM2 as
the coordination node and VM1–VM3 as executor-bearing workers [10]. Apache Flink offers
strong stateful processing for bounded and unbounded streams [2], but the implemented
workloads are primarily controlled batch and SQL transformations executed through
reproducible Airflow workflows. Spark therefore provides the required distribution without

introducing a second processing model that the project does not need.
Trino provides a separate distributed SQL access layer over the Iceberg and Hive-governed
lakehouse [30]. This separation prevents analytical exploration from being represented
merely as another transformation job and gives the deployment an explicit coordina-

Great Expectations turns data quality into an explicit validation layer by applying
documented expectations and persisting validation results and Data Docs evidence [21].
Custom validation scripts could test individual rules, but they would require the project to
recreate result storage, structured suites, and human-readable evidence. The retained tool
therefore supports both operational checking and the report’s need for verifiable quality
artifacts.
Airflow orchestrates the implemented sequence of environment reset, lakehouse setup,
service readiness, the integrated Kafka–HDFS–Spark raw-loading and raw-quality stage,
and dbt-spark transformation [1]. Prefect and Dagster provide credible Python-oriented and
asset-oriented orchestration models [28, 14]; however, Airflow’s explicit DAG representation,
scheduling, retries, task logs, and run history already align with the project’s five-workflow
execution contract.
Prometheus collects platform and exporter metrics, Grafana presents them as operational
dashboards, and cAdvisor contributes container-level visibility from the VM nodes [29,

it provides a centralized view of infrastructure health and pipeline behavior across the
control and data planes.

Docker packages and isolates the services deployed on the control plane and VM nodes [17].
Containerization does not make the platform distributed by itself; rather, it makes the

IV.11 Design Rationale and Trade-offs
The final design reflects deliberate engineering trade-offs rather than maximum-complexity

V.6 Hive Metastore and Catalog Governance
Hive Metastore is implemented as the shared metadata authority of the lakehouse platform.
It is deployed on VM2 together with its PostgreSQL backing store and acts as the central
catalog reference for upper services such as Spark and Trino [4, 6].
Its role in the implementation is essential: once Spark writes the raw Iceberg tables, their
definitions must be discoverable through a common metadata layer. Without this shared
governance service, each engine would have to manage table visibility independently,
weakening consistency across the platform.
In practical terms, Hive Metastore allows the platform to:
•register the lakehouse tables created after Spark loading;
•expose the same logical objects to different access engines;
• preserve a governed catalog boundary between raw persistence and analytical con-
sumption.
This makes the metadata layer a real implementation component, not a theoretical addition.
It is the reason the lakehouse can behave like a coordinated data platform rather than a
simple collection of files.

VI.5 Distributed Spark Execution
Spark provides the principal distributed-processing mechanism of the platform. The Spark
master is hosted on VM2, while executor capacity is registered through workers on VM1,
VM2, and VM3. Processing capacity is therefore not bound to the coordination node.
The Spark master interface exposes the registered worker pool, and applications allocate
executors according to workload requirements and available capacity. Processing tasks can
consequently be scheduled beyond the coordinator node itself; the corresponding runtime
evidence is examined in Chapter VIII.
The deployment also includes a Spark submit client, Spark Thrift Server, and Spark
History Server on VM2. These components preserve the separation between cluster entry,
SQL access, execution, and historical inspection [10]:
• Spark jobs are launched from the control plane through an SSH-based remote submis-
sion path to VM2;
• transformation-oriented SQL access targets the distributed Spark environment through
the Thrift interface;
•application history is retained through the History Server for later inspection;
• execution is coordinated centrally while tasks are carried out by workload-selected
workers.
Executor allocation is workload-dependent. A registered worker may receive no tasks
during a light application, because distributed execution does not imply equal simultaneous
utilization of every node. The relevant criterion is that the cluster can allocate work beyond
VM2 when the workload and available resources require it.

storage, transport, compute, and query-worker capacity against those shared services.

firmed, preventing Airflow and the monitoring stack from interpreting normal initialization
delays as operational failures. This sequence follows service dependencies rather than an
arbitrary launch order.

VI.7 Conclusion
This chapter clarified that the deployment of AdOptimizer CDP is intentionally distributed

Chapter VII

VII.1 Introduction
This chapter presents the security architecture integrated into AdOptimizer CDP. The
goal is not to claim a complete enterprise identity-management or zero-trust program,
but to provide an implemented hardening baseline for service identity, browser access,
exposed interfaces, remote operations, credential separation, and persisted runtime data.
The controls are grounded in established secure-service mechanisms [26, 3, 27, 13].
VII.2 Security Objectives
The security work of the project follows six main objectives:
•authenticate critical distributed services rather than relying on implicit trust;
•secure browser-facing administrative or analytical interfaces;
•protect exposed endpoints through encryption in transit;
•reduce the operational risk of remote orchestration actions;
•separate service, administration, and demonstration identities and credentials;
•strengthen the protection of service data persisted on the virtual machines.

VII.3 Kerberos-Secured Hadoop Services
Kerberos is the core identity mechanism used to secure the Hadoop side of the platform.
In a distributed environment, services such as the NameNode and DataNodes should not
trust requests only because they originate from another cluster component. They must
validate the identity of the caller explicitly [26, 3].
In the implementation, the Kerberos KDC and administration service are hosted on VM2,
which is already the coordination-heavy node of the data plane. This is consistent with
the overall architecture: the node that centralizes namespace and metadata authority also
centralizes identity management for the secure Hadoop runtime.
The practical contribution of Kerberos in this project is threefold:
• service principals and keytabs provide authenticated identities for non-interactive
Hadoop services;
•it allows secure service-to-service interactions in the distributed cluster;
• it provides the foundation for browser-based negotiated authentication through SP-
NEGO.
The Hadoop runtime therefore uses explicit authentication rather than relying only on
open internal trust between cluster components.

The HDFS browser path is protected through SPNEGO layered on top of the Kerberos-

It becomes part of the authenticated architecture [3, 26].

The implementation required both server-side and client-side alignment:
•server-side Kerberos-aware Hadoop configuration;
•SPNEGO-protected web endpoints;
•HTTPS exposure of the HDFS browser interfaces;
•MIT Kerberos client configuration and a valid ticket on the Windows workstation;
• Firefox SPNEGO configuration aligned with the documented project hostname aliases
rather than raw IP navigation.

SPNEGO failure must therefore be distinguished from the health of the Kerberos-secured

VII.6 Authenticated Trino Access
Trino is not exposed as an open analytical endpoint. Instead, the project implements an
authenticated gateway in front of the coordinator-side browser access path. This has two
important effects [30]:
•it reduces uncontrolled direct access to the analytical query service;
•it applies an explicit authentication boundary to user-facing SQL access.
The gateway is hosted on VM2 together with the Trino coordinator, which is consistent
with the node’s role as the entry point for coordination-heavy services. Through this
design, the browser-facing access path is separated from the internal worker topology.
Users connect to the protected entry point, while the internal distributed Trino execution
still takes place through the coordinator and worker nodes.
Authentication is enforced at the gateway level; this control should not be interpreted as a
complete enterprise IAM or fine-grained authorization framework.

VII.7 SSH-Based Remote Spark Submission
The orchestration model relies on a remote Spark submission path from the control plane

through uncontrolled or manually repeated remote actions. The platform therefore uses
an SSH-based remote execution path with dedicated key material and documented access
preparation [27].
This design improves the platform in three ways:
•it keeps the control plane separate from the heavy Spark runtime;
•it avoids exposing the orchestration flow through weaker manual execution habits;
• it provides a consistent operational bridge between local Airflow logic and the dis-

Operational Identity and Credential Separation
The deployment separates non-interactive service identities from human-operated admin-

sharing and clarifies which identity is used at each access boundary.
The implemented separation is a baseline control rather than a centralized secret-
management system. Automated secret rotation, enterprise vault integration, and
organization-wide identity federation remain outside the current scope.

Chapter IX
Discussion and Critical Analysis
IX.1 Introduction
Chapter VIII established that AdOptimizer CDP operated as an integrated distributed
customer-data platform. The purpose of the present chapter is not to repeat those validation
results, but to interpret their significance. The discussion examines the coherence of the
architectural choices, the trade-offs introduced by the selected stack and deployment model,
the lessons derived from implementation, and the limits within which the observed results
remain valid.
The analysis follows the principles commonly associated with distributed data-intensive
systems and lakehouse architectures: explicit responsibility boundaries, durable data,
independent compute and storage concerns, shared metadata, reproducible processing,
and controlled operational exposure [22, 11]. The conclusions are deliberately restricted to
the implemented three-VM data plane, the local control plane, and the retained evidence
presented in the preceding chapter.
IX.2 Critical Assessment of the Architectural Deci-
sions
The platform was designed as a chain of cooperating capabilities rather than as a single
processing application. This separation made it possible to assign ingestion, persistence,
computation, cataloging, transformation, validation, orchestration, observation, and pro-
tection to explicit architectural layers. The resulting modularity is valuable because a
change in one concern does not necessarily require redesigning the entire platform. However,
modularity also creates more interfaces, configuration contracts, and runtime dependencies
that must remain mutually consistent.

IX.3.1 End-to-End Coherence
The implementation connects the complete data lifecycle. The same three source
perspectives—campaign response, digital behavior, and retail transactions—move through
transport, bronze persistence, raw lakehouse loading, raw-data validation, modeled trans-

IX.3.2 Distributed Execution with Shared Governance
The deployment demonstrates distribution in the areas where it is technically meaningful:
Kafka brokers span the three virtual machines, HDFS stores data through three DataNodes,
Spark registers workers on all three machines, and Trino separates its coordinator from
two dedicated workers. At the same time, Hive Metastore and Iceberg provide a shared
table interpretation across processing and query services. This combination is important
because distribution without consistent metadata would only move computation across
nodes; it would not create a governed analytical platform [4, 6].
IX.3.3 Reproducibility and Evidence
Readiness checks, ordered orchestration, dbt tests, Great Expectations artifacts, Airflow
histories, and monitoring dashboards make execution outcomes inspectable. The platform
therefore preserves evidence not only of resulting data, but also of the operational path that
produced it. This strengthens reproducibility and separates a demonstrable engineering
result from an implementation that can only be explained from source code or manual
observation.
IX.3.4 Security-Aware Operational Design
Security was integrated into deployed paths rather than left as a conceptual recom-
mendation. Kerberos-secured Hadoop interactions, SPNEGO/HTTPS browser access,
authenticated gateways, SSH-based submission, and separated operational identities re-
duce the openness of the baseline cluster. Their value lies in demonstrating how security
affects service communication and deployment practice. The result remains infrastruc-
ture hardening rather than a complete enterprise identity-governance system, but it is
nevertheless part of the functioning architecture.
IX.4 Trade-Offs and Operational Constraints
IX.4.1 Resource Capacity versus Architectural Breadth
The three virtual machines provide sufficient separation to demonstrate distributed be-
havior, but their limited CPU, memory, and storage constrain concurrency and workload
scale. The breadth of the stack also means that infrastructure resources are shared by
storage, processing, query, metadata, security, and monitoring services. Consequently,

the deployment proves architectural feasibility and integration; it does not establish the

IX.4.2 Distribution versus Centralization
Several worker-oriented capabilities are distributed, while coordination remains intention-
ally centralized. VM2 hosts central roles including the HDFS NameNode, Spark master,
Hive Metastore, and Trino coordinator, and the local machine hosts Airflow and the
principal monitoring services. This arrangement is appropriate for a resource-constrained
implementation, but the loss of a coordinator host or the control plane would interrupt
important platform capabilities. The deployment must therefore be described as multi-node
and distributed, not as fully redundant or highly available.
IX.4.3 Specialized Components versus Integration Cost
Each selected component has a clear responsibility, yet every boundary creates an integra-
tion obligation. File paths, schemas, catalog identifiers, Kerberos principals, certificates,
hostnames, ports, and service readiness must agree across independently configured sys-
tems. Much of the engineering difficulty consequently lies between tools rather than inside
them. This is a characteristic trade-off of distributed data platforms: specialized services
improve separation of concerns while increasing the cost of maintaining reliable interfaces
[22].
IX.4.4 Controlled Batch Processing versus Real-Time Semantics
Kafka provides event transport, but the validated lifecycle is an orchestrated sequence in
which the raw-loading workflow includes explicit quality tasks and is followed by a governed
transformation workflow. The implementation should therefore not be interpreted as a
continuously updated real-time customer profile or an event-driven personalization engine.
Its current strength is controlled, replayable, and observable batch-oriented processing
over heterogeneous customer datasets.
IX.4.5 Hardening versus Operational Simplicity
Kerberos tickets, keytabs, SPNEGO client settings, TLS trust, gateways, and optional
encrypted runtime storage improve the security posture while making deployment and
troubleshooting more sensitive. Internal or self-signed trust paths require deliberate client
configuration, gateway authentication is not equivalent to enterprise IAM, and LUKS

layer from being overstated while preserving its implemented value.
IX.5 Engineering Lessons Learned
Several lessons emerged from constructing and validating the platform.
1. Interfaces are as important as individual services.Successful component
startup is insufficient when schemas, metadata, identities, or network assumptions
disagree across service boundaries.
2. Readiness must precede data movement.Separating environment preparation
and lakehouse readiness from data-processing workflows reduces ambiguous failures
and makes execution order reproducible.
3. Raw durability and analytical modeling serve different purposes.HDFS
bronze artifacts preserve replayability, while Iceberg, dbt-spark, and the curated
layers provide governed analytical structure. Combining these responsibilities would
weaken both.
4. Validation evidence is a platform output.Great Expectations results, dbt tests,
workflow histories, and monitoring metrics are not auxiliary screenshots; they are
retained proof that the platform executed its contracts.
5. Security changes architecture.Authentication, protected browser paths, remote
submission, and credential separation affect hostnames, protocols, startup order, and
operational procedures. They are most effective when designed with the deployment
rather than added after it.
6. A distributed demonstration still requires explicit claim boundaries.Multi-
node participation proves that work crosses machine boundaries, but it does not by
itself prove high availability, production capacity, or indefinite fault tolerance.

Academically, the contribution lies in the integration and validation of concepts that are

X.1 General Conclusion
Customer-related information is frequently fragmented across campaign systems, digital
interaction channels, and transactional applications. This fragmentation weakens replaya-
bility, governance, analytical trust, and the ability to prepare reliable inputs for advanced
customer-oriented services. The central problem addressed by this project was therefore to
determine how heterogeneous customer data could be integrated through a distributed,
governed, observable, and protected engineering platform rather than through isolated
scripts or a single local database.
To answer this problem, the project designed, implemented, deployed, and validated
AdOptimizer CDP within the broader AdOptimizer AI initiative at SMART AUTOMA-
TION TECHNOLOGIES. The resulting platform connects Kafka ingestion, HDFS bronze
persistence, Spark processing, Iceberg table management, Hive Metastore governance,
Trino analytical access, dbt-spark transformations, Great Expectations validation, Airflow
orchestration, and Prometheus/Grafana observability. These capabilities operate across a
local control plane and a three-VM Linux data plane, with security controls integrated
into the deployed access and execution paths.
The implementation demonstrates that campaign-response, digital-behavior, and retail-
transaction datasets can follow one reproducible lifecycle: source publication, replayable raw
landing, distributed loading, integrated raw-data validation, governed transformation and
testing, and external SQL consumption. The five-workflow orchestration sequence—reset,
setup, readiness, raw loading with embedded quality tasks, and dbt transformation—
together with retained validation artifacts and monitoring evidence shows that the result
is an operational platform rather than a collection of independently demonstrated tech-
nologies.

X.3 Future Perspectives
The next developments should preserve the current architecture’s separation of concerns
while increasing its availability, data coverage, governance maturity, and downstream
business value. The perspectives can be organized into three successive directions.
X.3.1 Platform Industrialization
The first priority is to strengthen the operating foundation before expanding business
functionality. Central coordinator and catalog responsibilities should be made more resilient,
and the local control plane should evolve toward a managed deployment environment.
Backup, restoration, failover, and node-loss scenarios should be tested explicitly rather
than inferred from successful nominal execution.
Deployment automation can also be extended through versioned environment configuration,
infrastructure provisioning, compatibility checks, and repeatable certificate and service-
identity initialization. Centralized secret management, automated certificate rotation,
finer-grained access control, and encryption enabled consistently across persistent runtime
storage would move the current security baseline closer to enterprise operation.
Performance evaluation should become systematic. Repeated benchmarks over identical
datasets and workflows can compare local and distributed execution, measure end-to-end
duration and service-level throughput, and identify CPU, memory, storage, and network
bottlenecks. Such measurements would guide capacity planning without replacing the
functional evidence already established by the project.
X.3.2 Customer 360 Enrichment
The second direction is to broaden the customer-data foundation. Additional operational
sources can extend the current campaign, behavioral, and transactional perspectives with
support interactions, consent and preference data, product usage, and other company-
relevantchannels.Eachnewsourceshouldretainthesameingestion,bronze,transformation,
quality, and evidence contracts.
A mature Customer 360 layer would also require canonical identifiers, controlled cross-
source identity resolution, survivorship rules, and explicit handling of uncertain matches.
Richer semantic marts, documented data contracts, lineage, retention rules, and access
policies would turn the current analytical foundation into a more complete governed

X.3.3 AdOptimizer AI Capabilities
The third direction concerns the intelligent services of the broader AdOptimizer AI vision.
Curated customer features can support segmentation, persona generation, churn-risk
estimation, customer-lifetime-value modeling, conversion analysis, campaign optimization,

Moving from analytical features to operational AI would require a dedicated lifecycle
